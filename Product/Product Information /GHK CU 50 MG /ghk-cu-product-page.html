What Is GHK-Cu?

A naturally occurring copper-binding tripeptide with a surprisingly wide range of biological activity.

GHK-Cu was first identified by Dr. Loren Pickart in the 1970s in human plasma. It's made up of three amino acids — Glycine, Histidine, and Lysine — which together form a specific binding pocket for copper ions (Cu²⁺). The resulting complex is highly stable (binding constant ~10¹⁶) and characteristic blue-green in color.

Despite being a small tripeptide, GHK-Cu has been found to influence the expression of hundreds of genes, regulate extracellular matrix protease activity, modulate inflammatory signaling, and support tissue repair processes. Natural plasma levels peak in early adulthood and decline progressively with age — a pattern that has driven substantial research into its role in tissue maintenance and age-related changes.

GHK-Cu delivers copper to cells in a controlled way, activating copper-dependent enzymes while avoiding the cellular toxicity associated with free copper ions. Beyond copper delivery, the peptide itself appears to engage multiple intracellular pathways, giving it an unusually broad range of effects relative to its size.

Structural Notes

The N-terminal glycine amino group and histidine imidazole nitrogen serve as the primary Cu²⁺ coordination sites, with additional coordination completing the square planar geometry. This specific coordination geometry is what gives GHK-Cu its characteristic color and biological activity — free GHK without copper shows reduced potency in most studied applications.

The extremely high binding constant (~10¹⁶) means copper stays coordinated under physiological conditions, allowing targeted delivery to tissues while preventing free copper toxicity. This stability also contributes to the peptide's resistance to peptidase degradation when in the copper-bound form.

Published Research Overview

A selection of research areas and findings from peer-reviewed studies on GHK-Cu.

Wound Healing
Topical GHK-Cu vs. Zinc Oxide in Open-Wound Models (Cangul et al.)
In rabbit open-wound models, topical GHK-Cu produced significantly smaller unhealed wound areas by day 7 (2.76 cm² vs. 3.22 cm² for zinc oxide). Wound contraction at day 7 was 30.96% in the GHK-Cu group vs. 19.38% for zinc oxide. Histological analysis showed significantly higher vascularization on day 14 (vessel count 5.35 vs. 1.92 for zinc oxide). Average full epithelialization time was 18.4 days for GHK-Cu vs. 21.5 for zinc oxide, and the untreated group had not fully healed by day 21.
Acute Lung Injury
GHK-Cu in LPS-Induced Acute Lung Injury (Park et al.)
In macrophage cultures, GHK-Cu pretreatment at 1–10 µM reversed LPS-induced ROS increases and restored SOD activity. TNF-α and IL-6 secretion were both significantly reduced. In vivo in mice with LPS-induced ALI, GHK-Cu pretreatment (10 µg/g) reduced lung injury scores, restored GSH and SOD activity, lowered inflammatory cytokines in lung fluid, and reduced neutrophil infiltration. The mechanism involved suppression of NF-κB p65 phosphorylation and p38 MAPK activation — with no effect on ERK1/2.
Neuroprotection
Intranasal GHK in Alzheimer's Mouse Model (Tucker et al.)
In 5xFAD Alzheimer's mice, intranasal GHK peptide treatment improved cognitive performance, reduced amyloid plaque burden, and lowered neuroinflammation markers. Researchers identified GHK-Cu's capacity to prevent copper- and zinc-induced protein aggregation as a mechanism potentially relevant to conditions where metal ion imbalance promotes toxic protein clustering.
Gene Modulation
GHK-Cu and Cancer Cell Gene Expression (Pickart & Margolina)
In MCF7 breast cancer and PC3 prostate cancer cell lines, GHK-Cu reversed pathological gene expression patterns associated with cancer progression. Separately, it shifted gene expression in metastasis-prone colon cancer cells toward healthier profiles, and demonstrated the ability to reverse the COPD-associated gene signature in lung tissue toward one of active repair and remodeling.

Storage & Reconstitution
Visual check: the blue-green color of the powder and reconstituted solution confirms the copper complex is intact. If solution appears colorless, verify pH and storage conditions.

Lyophilized Powder — Storage

  • Store at -20°C to -80°C in the original sealed vial
  • Keep away from light and moisture at all times
  • Stable for 24+ months under proper conditions
  • Blue-green color of the powder confirms copper complex integrity
  • Do not temperature-cycle sealed vials unnecessarily

Reconstitution

  • Use sterile water or PBS; maintain pH between 6.0 and 7.5
  • Add solvent slowly down the inside wall of the vial — not directly onto the powder
  • Swirl gently for 1–2 minutes until fully dissolved; do not shake
  • Solution should appear clear to light blue-green when ready
  • Avoid strongly acidic conditions (pH below 5) — these can dissociate the copper from the peptide
  • Also avoid high chelator concentrations in the buffer, which can compete for copper binding

Reconstituted Solution — Storage

  • Short-term: refrigerate at 4°C for up to 7–10 days
  • Long-term: freeze at -20°C in single-use aliquots
  • Prefer refrigeration over freezing for active use — repeated freeze-thaw affects stability
  • Maintain pH 6.0–7.5 in storage; use aliquots from a fresh tube for each experiment where possible

Safety & Waste Handling

  • Wear lab coat, gloves, and safety glasses when handling
  • Work in a well-ventilated area or under a fume hood
  • Dispose of waste according to local regulations for both biological materials and copper-containing compounds
  • Consult the Material Safety Data Sheet (MSDS) for full handling details

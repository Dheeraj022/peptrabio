What Is VIP?

A multisystem regulatory neuropeptide with roles spanning immunity, neuroscience, chronobiology, and vascular biology.

Vasoactive Intestinal Peptide was first isolated in 1970 from porcine small intestine, and its original name reflects what researchers saw first — powerful vasodilation. That turned out to be just one piece of a much larger story. Over the following five decades, VIP was found in neural fibres throughout the gut, brain, lungs, and immune tissue, and its receptor targets — VPAC1 and VPAC2 — were found to be expressed across nearly every major organ system.

VIP belongs to the secretin/glucagon superfamily, which means it shares structural and evolutionary relationships with peptides including PACAP, secretin, glucagon, GIP, GLP-1, and PHI. These structural similarities produce overlapping receptor pharmacology that makes VIP useful for comparative studies — researchers can use it alongside or in contrast to PACAP and secretin to dissect receptor subtype-specific effects across this peptide family.

In practice, VIP is used in the laboratory as a probe for six broad research areas: neuroprotection and neuroinflammation, circadian rhythm regulation in the suprachiasmatic nucleus, immune cell modulation, cardiovascular vasodilation, gastrointestinal motility, and respiratory airway function. What makes it particularly interesting as a research tool is that these effects are not mediated through a single dominant mechanism — VIP's VPAC1 and VPAC2 receptors have different tissue distributions, and the downstream consequences of activating them depend heavily on the cell type being studied.

VPAC Receptor Comparison
VPAC1 (VIPR1)
Broadly expressed across the CNS, peripheral organs, and immune cells. Primary mediator of VIP's anti-inflammatory effects on T cells, macrophages, and dendritic cells. Kd ≈1 nM. Also binds PACAP with similar affinity.
VPAC2 (VIPR2)
Concentrated in the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN), smooth muscle, and pancreatic tissue. Primary mediator of VIP's circadian synchronisation function and bronchodilatory effects. Kd ≈1 nM. Also binds PACAP.

Both receptors couple to Gs proteins and raise intracellular cAMP as the primary second messenger. The tissue-specific distribution of each receptor is what determines which biological effect dominates in any given experimental context — VPAC1 drives most immune effects, VPAC2 drives circadian and smooth muscle effects.

Mechanism of Action

From receptor binding to downstream signalling cascades — how VIP produces effects across multiple biological systems.

Primary Signalling Cascade

VIP binds VPAC1 or VPAC2 on the surface of target cells, triggering Gs protein activation. This stimulates adenylate cyclase to convert ATP into cyclic AMP (cAMP). Elevated intracellular cAMP activates protein kinase A (PKA), which phosphorylates CREB (cAMP response element-binding protein), driving transcriptional changes in genes involved in inflammation, survival, and metabolism. PKA also directly phosphorylates ion channels and other effector proteins, producing rapid non-transcriptional effects such as smooth muscle relaxation and ion transport changes.

Secondary Pathways

In some cell types and at higher concentrations, VPAC receptor activation also engages phospholipase C (producing IP3 and DAG, and mobilising intracellular calcium), MAPK/ERK pathways, and PI3K/Akt signalling. These secondary cascades contribute to VIP's effects on cell proliferation, differentiation, and survival — effects that are not fully accounted for by cAMP alone. Researchers designing experiments to study specific downstream pathways should use appropriate pathway inhibitors to distinguish primary from secondary signalling contributions.

Anti-Inflammatory Mechanism in More Detail

VIP's immunosuppressive action is well characterised and operates through multiple parallel routes. On the transcription factor side, cAMP/PKA signalling inhibits NF-κB nuclear translocation — suppressing the expression of TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-12. Simultaneously, VIP promotes STAT6 phosphorylation and STAT1 dephosphorylation, shifting cytokine output toward the anti-inflammatory IL-4/IL-10 axis. On T cells specifically, VPAC1 activation suppresses TCR-driven proliferation and promotes the differentiation of regulatory T cells (Tregs) rather than effector Th1 cells. The combined result is a coordinated shift away from pro-inflammatory immune activation without causing broad immunosuppression.

Research Applications

VIP's broad receptor distribution makes it useful across six distinct research domains.

🧠
Neuroprotection & CNS
Protects dopaminergic and other neurons from excitotoxicity, oxidative stress, and microglial-mediated inflammatory damage. Studied in Alzheimer's, Parkinson's, and MS models.
🕐
Circadian Rhythm Research
Essential synchroniser of SCN neuronal oscillators via VPAC2. Studied via SCN electrophysiology, wheel-running activity, and clock gene expression profiling.
🛡️
Immune Modulation
Suppresses Th1 responses, promotes Treg differentiation, inhibits NF-κB and TNF-α. Used in autoimmune models: colitis, arthritis, EAE, type 1 diabetes.
🫀

Potent vasodilator via cAMP and nitric oxide in coronary, cerebral, and pulmonary vessels. Used in ischaemia-reperfusion, blood pressure, and endothelial function studies.

NANC neurotransmitter providing bronchodilatory and anti-inflammatory tone. Studied in asthma, COPD, pulmonary fibrosis, and pulmonary hypertension models.
🫃
Gastrointestinal Research
Major enteric nervous system neurotransmitter. Regulates sphincter relaxation, pancreatic secretion, intestinal motility, and microbiome-immune communication.

Pharmacokinetic Design Considerations

VIP's plasma half-life of less than two minutes is one of the most practically important facts about this peptide for experimental design. In cell culture (in vitro) work, this is less of an issue because the enzymatic degradation environment is minimal. But in in vivo animal studies, a single bolus intravenous injection will produce only transient measurable effects lasting a few minutes at most. Researchers studying sustained physiological responses must use continuous infusion protocols or repeated dosing. When designing sampling timepoints, they need to align collection windows with the dosing schedule rather than assuming a prolonged active period. The short half-life can also be used advantageously in experiments requiring precise temporal control — the effect window shuts off quickly when dosing stops.

Storage & Reconstitution
⚡ Key handling notes for VIP VIP is more handling-sensitive than most peptides in this catalogue. The combination of photosensitivity, Met17 oxidation risk, and strong plastic adsorption tendency means each step below matters more than it would for a structurally simpler peptide.

Lyophilized Powder — Storage

  • Primary storage at –80°C for long-term stability; –20°C acceptable for shorter-term use
  • Protect rigorously from light — keep in original packaging or opaque container at all times
  • Store in a dry, desiccated environment; inert gas (nitrogen or argon) atmosphere for long-term storage
  • Stable for 12+ months at –80°C when sealed correctly
  • Minimise time at room temperature during each handling session

Reconstitution

  • Use sterile water, bacteriostatic water, PBS, or HEPES buffer; maintain pH 7.0–7.4
  • Add solvent slowly down the inner wall of the vial — do not jet directly onto the powder to prevent foaming
  • Swirl gently at 4°C; full dissolution typically takes 2–5 minutes; never shake or vortex
  • Add 0.1% BSA to working solutions when using low peptide concentrations to prevent adsorption losses
  • Consider protease inhibitors for extended cell culture incubations where media enzymes could degrade VIP over time

After Reconstitution

  • Use within 48 hours when stored at 4°C; fresh preparation is preferred for each experiment
  • For longer storage, freeze in single-use aliquots at –80°C; limit freeze-thaw cycles to a maximum of 1–2
  • Use low-binding microcentrifuge tubes and pipette tips throughout
  • Pre-rinse all containers with BSA solution or VIP working solution to saturate non-specific binding sites before adding experimental doses
  • Minimise oxygen exposure to protect the Met17 residue from oxidation

## What Is VIP?

Vasoactive Intestinal Peptide — VIP for short — is a 28-amino acid neuropeptide that belongs to the secretin and glucagon peptide superfamily, a family of structurally related regulatory hormones that includes secretin, glucagon, PACAP (pituitary adenylate cyclase-activating polypeptide), and PHI (peptide histidine isoleucine). It was first isolated in 1970 by Said and Mutt from porcine small intestinal tissue, where its original characterisation focused on its potent vasodilatory activity. That first impression turned out to be only a narrow slice of VIP's biological portfolio.

Over the following decades, research revealed VIP to be one of the most broadly distributed and functionally versatile regulatory peptides in mammalian biology. It is found in neurons throughout the central and peripheral nervous systems, in the enteric nervous system of the gut wall, in respiratory tract neural fibres, in immune cells, and in multiple endocrine and exocrine glands. Its effects span smooth muscle relaxation, fluid secretion, immune cell modulation, neuronal protection, circadian rhythm synchronisation, and vascular regulation — making it unusually valuable as a laboratory research tool for studying how the body integrates information across multiple physiological systems simultaneously.

VIP exerts most of its known effects through two high-affinity G-protein-coupled receptors, VPAC1 and VPAC2, both of which signal primarily through the Gs/adenylate cyclase pathway, raising intracellular cyclic AMP (cAMP) as the central second messenger. The tissue distribution of these two receptor subtypes determines which biological effects dominate in any given organ or cell type, which is why VIP research spans such a wide range of disciplines.

## Structural Features Worth Understanding

### Why the C-Terminal Amide Matters

The –NH₂ group at the C-terminus of VIP is not simply a synthetic artefact — it is an absolute requirement for productive receptor engagement. When the C-terminal is left as a free carboxylic acid instead, receptor binding affinity drops by orders of magnitude. This is why VIP must be synthesised and verified with correct amidation, and why the COA specifically confirms this modification for every batch.

### The Amphipathic Helix

In environments that mimic the lipid-rich surface of a cell membrane — which is where receptor binding actually occurs — VIP folds into a partially alpha-helical conformation. The N-terminal stretch (roughly residues 1–14) forms what is called an amphipathic helix: one face is hydrophobic and the other is hydrophilic. This structural arrangement is important for how the peptide approaches and engages its receptor. The C-terminal region contributes additional contacts that help determine whether VPAC1 or VPAC2 is preferentially activated in a given context.

### The Methionine Vulnerability

VIP contains only one sulphur-bearing residue — methionine at position 17. Under oxidising conditions, Met17 can be converted to methionine sulfoxide, which alters the peptide's secondary structure and weakens receptor binding. This is the main reason VIP requires more careful handling than peptides with no oxidisable side chains. Working solutions should be prepared fresh, stored away from air, and used promptly. Antioxidants can be added to buffer solutions in some applications to extend working life.

### Why VIP Sticks to Plastic

The high density of basic residues (three lysines, two arginines, one histidine) gives VIP a net positive charge at physiological pH. Negatively charged surfaces — including many standard laboratory plastics — can bind VIP non-specifically, reducing the effective concentration delivered to cells. This is why low-binding tubes and pre-rinsing containers with BSA solution are recommended when working with VIP at low concentrations.

## Receptor Pharmacology

### VPAC1 (VIPR1)

VPAC1 is broadly expressed across the central nervous system, peripheral organs, and immune cell populations. It binds VIP with high affinity (Kd ≈1 nM) and also recognises PACAP with comparable affinity, which makes careful experimental design important when trying to isolate VIP-specific effects. Receptor activation drives Gs protein coupling, adenylate cyclase stimulation, and cAMP production. In immune cells, this cAMP elevation is the primary mechanism behind VIP's anti-inflammatory effects — it activates PKA, which phosphorylates CREB and modulates the transcription of cytokine genes.

### VPAC2 (VIPR2)

VPAC2 shares the same basic signalling mechanism as VPAC1 but has a more restricted expression pattern. It is concentrated in the suprachiasmatic nucleus (the brain's circadian pacemaker), in smooth muscle tissue throughout the body, and in the pancreas. VPAC2's SCN expression is what makes it central to VIP's role in circadian rhythm synchronisation — a function that is essentially lost in VPAC2 knockout animals. The same receptor also mediates VIP's bronchodilatory effects in airway smooth muscle and its cardiovascular smooth muscle relaxation.

### Secondary Signalling Pathways

Beyond the dominant Gs/cAMP axis, both VPAC receptors can activate additional pathways in a cell-type and concentration-dependent fashion. These include phospholipase C activation (producing IP3 and DAG, and mobilising intracellular calcium), MAPK/ERK pathway engagement, and PI3K/Akt signalling. These secondary cascades contribute to VIP's effects on cell survival, proliferation, and differentiation — effects that are not fully explained by cAMP alone and that become more prominent at higher peptide concentrations in some experimental systems.

## Research Applications

### Neuroprotection and CNS Research

VIP provides meaningful neuroprotection in multiple in vitro and in vivo model systems. In neuronal cultures, it protects dopaminergic neurons and other cell types from damage caused by glutamate excitotoxicity, oxidative stress, and inflammatory mediators from activated microglia. The protective mechanism runs through VPAC receptor activation, cAMP elevation, and PKA-mediated phosphorylation of pro-survival transcription factors. VIP also directly suppresses microglial activation, which is important because overactivated microglia are a significant driver of collateral neuronal damage in neuroinflammatory conditions. In the context of neurodegenerative disease research, VIP is studied in Alzheimer's disease models (protection against amyloid-beta toxicity), Parkinson's disease models (dopaminergic neuron survival), and EAE models of multiple sclerosis.

### Circadian Rhythm and Chronobiology

This is arguably VIP's most specialised and well-characterised research application. The suprachiasmatic nucleus contains a population of VIP-expressing neurons that are essential for coordinating the individual molecular clocks running inside each of the thousands of SCN neurons. Without VIP signalling through VPAC2, these individual oscillators lose synchrony with each other and with the external light-dark cycle — the result is a dispersed, incoherent rhythmic output that cannot drive normal body-wide circadian organisation. Research in this area uses SCN electrophysiology, wheel-running activity monitoring, circadian gene expression profiling (measuring Period, Cryptochrome, BMAL1, and Clock across time), and genetically modified models to dissect exactly how VIP maintains the coherent output of the circadian pacemaker.

### Anti-Inflammatory and Immune Research

VIP acts on VPAC1 and VPAC2 receptors expressed on T cells, macrophages, dendritic cells, and natural killer cells. On T cells, it suppresses proliferation, inhibits Th1 differentiation (reducing IFN-γ production), and promotes the development of regulatory T cells (Tregs) that secrete IL-10 and suppress excessive immune activation. On macrophages and dendritic cells, VIP inhibits NF-κB nuclear translocation and reduces the output of TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-12. The combination of these effects — dampening inflammatory effectors while promoting regulatory responses — makes VIP useful in models of autoimmune diseases including rheumatoid arthritis, inflammatory bowel disease, experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis, and type 1 diabetes.

### Cardiovascular and Vascular Research

VIP is one of the most potent endogenous vasodilators identified in mammalian physiology, producing dose-dependent relaxation of coronary, cerebral, pulmonary, and mesenteric arteries in isolated vessel preparations and whole-animal models. The vasodilatory mechanism works through two parallel routes: VPAC receptor-driven cAMP elevation in vascular smooth muscle reduces intracellular calcium and promotes relaxation directly, while VIP also stimulates endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) in vascular endothelium, generating nitric oxide that relaxes smooth muscle through the cGMP pathway. Research applications include ischaemia-reperfusion injury models, blood pressure regulation studies, coronary artery perfusion experiments, and investigations of neurovascular coupling in the brain.

### Gastrointestinal Research

VIP is a major neurotransmitter in the enteric nervous system, the extensive neural network embedded in the gut wall. In laboratory GI research, VIP is used to study smooth muscle relaxation in the oesophageal sphincter, gastric fundus, and intestinal wall; stimulation of pancreatic bicarbonate and water secretion; inhibition of gastric acid production; and promotion of intestinal mucus. More recent research has connected VIP levels in the gut to microbiome composition, suggesting VIP participates in bidirectional host-microbe communication affecting both motility and local immune responses. Research models range from isolated intestinal ring preparations to in vivo transit studies and enteric nerve electrophysiology.

### Respiratory System Research

In the airways, VIP functions as a non-adrenergic, non-cholinergic (NANC) neurotransmitter that provides bronchodilatory and anti-inflammatory tone. Laboratory studies use isolated tracheal ring preparations, precision-cut lung slices, and in vivo pulmonary function measurements to characterise VIP's relaxation of airway smooth muscle and its inhibition of airway inflammation. In pulmonary hypertension models, VIP has been shown to reduce pulmonary vascular resistance. Clinical data from patients with pulmonary arterial hypertension has documented reduced VIP expression in their lung tissue, which has supported continued interest in the peptide's role in this condition.

## Pharmacokinetic Profile

| Parameter | Detail |
|---|---|
| Plasma half-life | <2 minutes in circulation |
| Primary degradation enzymes | Neutral endopeptidase (NEP), DPP-IV, mast cell chymase |
| Main cleavage sites | Tyr10–Thr11 and Lys20–Lys21 bonds |
| Blood-brain barrier penetration | Limited (large, highly charged peptide) |
| Effective local concentration | Higher than systemic levels suggest due to paracrine release |
| In vivo dosing implication | Continuous infusion or repeated dosing required for sustained effects |

VIP's <2-minute plasma half-life is one of the most important practical considerations in experimental design. It means that single-bolus intravenous administration produces only very transient effects, and that sustained biological outcomes require either continuous infusion protocols or repeated dosing schedules. For in vitro work, this is less of a concern since enzymatic degradation is minimal in most cell culture media. For in vivo experiments, researchers need to carefully align their sampling timepoints with the dosing schedule to capture effects at meaningful biological windows.

## Storage and Reconstitution

### Lyophilized Powder

Store sealed vials at –80°C for long-term stability; –20°C is acceptable for shorter-term use. Protect vigorously from light — VIP is photosensitive. A dry, desiccated environment is essential; inert gas (nitrogen or argon) atmosphere is recommended for long-term storage. Minimise the time the vial spends at room temperature during each handling session.

### Reconstitution

Use sterile water, bacteriostatic water, PBS, or HEPES buffer. Maintain pH between 7.0 and 7.4 for optimal activity and stability. Add solvent slowly down the inside wall of the vial to prevent foaming and surface denaturation. Swirl gently at 4°C if needed — full dissolution typically takes 2–5 minutes. Do not shake or vortex.

Add BSA at 0.1% final concentration when working at low peptide concentrations to prevent adsorption losses. Consider protease inhibitors for extended cell culture incubations where enzymatic degradation in conditioned media could reduce effective concentration over time.

### After Reconstitution

Use within 48 hours when stored at 4°C. Prepare fresh solutions for each experiment whenever feasible. For longer storage, freeze in single-use aliquots at –80°C; limit freeze-thaw cycles to one or two maximum. Use low-binding microcentrifuge tubes and pipette tips; pre-rinse containers with BSA solution before adding VIP to saturate non-specific binding sites. Minimise oxygen exposure throughout to protect Met17 from oxidation.
